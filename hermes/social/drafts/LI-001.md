## Draft copy

A hydraulic system can look clean and still be operating under damaging contamination.

In construction, mining and other heavy-duty environments, contamination does not enter through one route. It can be present from assembly, enter through breathers and seals, be generated by normal component wear, or develop as the fluid degrades in service.

Once those particles circulate through pumps, valves and actuators, filtration stops being a simple maintenance item. It becomes part of the system's protection strategy.

The practical question is not only whether a filter is installed.

It is whether the hydraulic circuit is being kept within the cleanliness level required by the components it is protecting.

That is why standards such as ISO 4406 and ISO 16889 matter: one describes fluid cleanliness, while the other provides a standardized method for evaluating hydraulic filter performance.

Contamination control starts with understanding the condition of the fluid, the sensitivity of the components and the way contamination enters the system.
